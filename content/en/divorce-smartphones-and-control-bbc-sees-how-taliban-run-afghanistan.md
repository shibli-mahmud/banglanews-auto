The Taliban's grip on Afghanistan is tightening, not just through traditional enforcement but also through an intricate web of social and technological control. Five years on from their swift takeover, the group is actively molding the nation according to their interpretation of Islamic law, with profound implications for everyday life.

Senior Taliban figures, speaking to the BBC under condition of anonymity, paint a picture of a regime grappling with the realities of governance while adhering rigidly to their ideology. The strict enforcement of dress codes and the severe curtailment of women's rights remain central tenets. However, the narrative is not one of outright technological rejection. In a surprising twist, smartphones and social media are being actively utilized by the Taliban to disseminate their message, monitor dissent, and even manage administrative tasks. This duality – the suppression of freedoms alongside the strategic adoption of modern tools – highlights a pragmatic, albeit authoritarian, approach to power.

Interviews reveal a deep concern with maintaining societal order, often manifesting in extreme measures. The concept of divorce, for instance, is being heavily scrutinized and restricted, reflecting a desire to control family structures and uphold traditional values. This focus on social engineering extends to all facets of Afghan life, from education to public spaces. While the international community watches with apprehension, the Taliban are demonstrating a clear intent to cement their rule, employing a blend of ideological rigidity and surprising technological adaptation to achieve their vision for Afghanistan.
