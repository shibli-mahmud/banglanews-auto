Justice Secretary Dominic Raab has announced significant changes to early release schemes in England and Wales, aimed at preventing serious offenders, including those convicted of rape and child sexual abuse, from benefiting from automatic release before serving the entirety of their sentences. The move follows intense pressure from victims' families and campaigners, notably Lissie Harper, the widow of PC Andrew Harper, who was tragically killed in the line of duty in 2019.

Mrs. Harper has vociferously campaigned since her husband's death, highlighting the perceived injustice of those convicted of his murder being eligible for release under existing early release provisions. She has repeatedly expressed her dismay, calling the prospect 'disgusting' and arguing that it undermines the severity of their crimes and the gravity of their sentences. Her advocacy has been instrumental in bringing this issue to the forefront of public and political discourse.

The new government plans will introduce a statutory ban, ensuring that individuals convicted of the most serious sexual offenses, as well as those who commit murder, will no longer be automatically considered for release under the HDC (Home Detention Curfew) scheme. This scheme allows some prisoners to be released up to two months before the end of their sentence, subject to electronic tagging and other conditions. The government states this change is about ensuring that the most dangerous criminals serve longer in custody and to provide greater reassurance to the public.

While the specific legislation is yet to be fully detailed, the announcement has been met with widespread approval from victim support groups and those who have campaigned for stricter sentencing and release policies. It represents a significant shift in policy, directly addressing concerns that the current system disproportionately fails to reflect the severity of certain heinous crimes, particularly in cases involving extreme violence and sexual offenses against vulnerable individuals.
