Psychiatrists have issued a stark warning, stating that the National Health Service (NHS) is consistently failing adults who were subjected to child sexual abuse (CSA), leaving them to cope with the profound and often devastating consequences of their trauma years later. A recent, damning report, compiled by a coalition of mental health experts, reveals a pattern of "mistreatment and mismanagement" by services intended to provide support for survivors who come forward in adulthood.

The report, which draws on decades of anecdotal evidence and clinical experience, details how survivors often face significant barriers when seeking help. These include long waiting lists for therapy, a lack of specialized trauma-informed care, and insufficient training among healthcare professionals to recognise and effectively treat complex trauma. Many survivors report feeling re-traumatized by the very systems designed to help them, due to a lack of understanding, empathy, or appropriate therapeutic approaches.

Experts argue that this systemic failure has direct repercussions on the lives of survivors, contributing to a higher prevalence of mental health conditions such as complex post-traumatic stress disorder (C-PTSD), depression, anxiety, and difficulties with relationships and employment. The report emphasizes that while early intervention is crucial, adult survivors also require dedicated, long-term support that acknowledges the protracted nature of healing from CSA.

"We are seeing the legacy of childhood abuse playing out in adulthood because the NHS is not adequately equipped to deal with these complex needs," stated Dr. Eleanor Vance, a lead author of the report. "Survivors are often left feeling abandoned, unheard, and further damaged by the system. This is not just a failure of individual services, but a systemic issue that requires urgent attention and significant investment."

The report calls for a comprehensive overhaul of mental health services for CSA survivors, including increased funding for specialist trauma services, mandatory training for all NHS staff on recognising and responding to trauma, and the development of clear pathways for survivors to access appropriate care without further delay or distress. The authors stress that addressing this failure is not only a matter of clinical necessity but a moral imperative.
