A public inquiry has heard that allegations of war crimes against elite SAS soldiers were deliberately not reported to military police for fear of damaging troop morale. Evidence presented at the R v Stephenson and others inquiry suggests that operational figures actively chose to withhold information regarding alleged extra-judicial killings and other serious misconduct from the relevant authorities for a considerable period.

This approach, the inquiry heard, was driven by a desire to protect the reputation and perceived invincibility of the UK's most secretive military unit. Critics argue this policy created a dangerous culture of impunity, where serious allegations could go uninvestigated, potentially leaving victims without justice and allowing perpetrators to escape accountability. 

The failure to refer these grave accusations to military police meant that for years, the formal investigative bodies were unaware of the scale and nature of the alleged crimes. This delay has raised significant concerns about the thoroughness and impartiality of any subsequent investigations, and the potential for evidence to be lost or compromised over time. 

Lawyers representing victims' families have expressed outrage, stating that the decision to prioritise morale over justice for alleged atrocities is a profound betrayal of military values. The inquiry is expected to examine the chain of command and decision-making processes that allowed these allegations to be suppressed, and will likely make recommendations aimed at strengthening accountability mechanisms for special forces personnel in future operations. The implications for the UK's international standing and adherence to the laws of armed conflict are also under scrutiny.
