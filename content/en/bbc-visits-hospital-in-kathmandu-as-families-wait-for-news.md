The air inside the Tribhuvan University Teaching Hospital in Kathmandu is thick with a potent mix of hope and despair. Families, their faces etched with worry, huddle in corridors, their eyes fixed on any official or medical personnel who might offer news. Over 150 people have been confirmed dead following a catastrophic flash flood that swept through areas along the Nepal-Tibet border. Hundreds more remain missing, their fates unknown, fueling the agonizing wait for their loved ones.

Nurses and doctors move with a weary urgency, their efforts focused on treating the injured and comforting those who are searching. The sheer scale of the disaster has overwhelmed local resources, and the pleas for information are relentless. "We haven't heard anything since the news broke," a tearful woman named Sita told the BBC, her voice trembling as she clutched a faded photograph. "My brother was in the affected area. We just need to know if he's safe."

The hospital is serving as a focal point for the unfolding human tragedy. Families have travelled from remote villages, their journey marked by the desperate hope of finding their missing relatives amidst the casualty lists or among the recovering patients. The lack of clear communication and the sheer number of people affected have created an environment of profound uncertainty. International aid is beginning to arrive, but the immediate need is for information, for reassurance, and for any sign that those lost might still be found.
