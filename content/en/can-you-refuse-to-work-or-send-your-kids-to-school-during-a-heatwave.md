The rising mercury poses significant challenges, prompting questions about whether individuals can legally refuse to work or keep their children home from school.  In most jurisdictions, there isn't an automatic right to cease work or school attendance solely due to a heatwave, unless specific health and safety thresholds are breached or official advisories are issued.

For employees, workplace health and safety regulations often mandate that employers provide a safe working environment. If temperatures inside a workplace become dangerously high and exceed legally defined limits or create an unreasonable risk, employees may have grounds to raise concerns with their employer. Some contracts or union agreements might also include clauses addressing extreme weather conditions.  However, a blanket refusal to work without consulting an employer or seeking official guidance could have repercussions. It's advisable to report concerns to management and, if necessary, to relevant health and safety authorities.

Similarly, for school-aged children, school closures are typically declared by local education authorities based on severe weather, including extreme heat, when conditions are deemed unsafe for students and staff. This usually involves assessments of indoor temperatures, ventilation, and student well-being. Parents are generally expected to send their children to school unless an official closure is announced or there are specific medical reasons for absence, supported by a doctor's note.  Parents should stay informed through official school communications and local news for any announcements regarding closures or alternative arrangements. Prioritizing health and safety is paramount for both employees and families during these challenging climatic conditions.
