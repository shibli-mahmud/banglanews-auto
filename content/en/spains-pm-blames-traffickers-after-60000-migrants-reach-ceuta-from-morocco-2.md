Madrid is grappling with an unprecedented influx of migrants after an estimated 60,000 people, many of them Moroccan youths, managed to cross the border into the Spanish North African enclave of Ceuta. The breach, which occurred over a period of approximately 24 hours, saw thousands swimming or wading across the shallow maritime border from neighboring Morocco.  Spanish authorities were caught largely off guard by the sheer scale and speed of the arrivals.

Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez, cutting short a trip to Andalusia, stated that the unprecedented surge was the result of "blackmail" and the actions of "irresponsible" traffickers. He emphasized that the Spanish government would work to restore order and secure the border, while also reiterating Spain's commitment to its international responsibilities. The situation has placed a significant strain on Ceuta's limited infrastructure and humanitarian services. Many of the arrivals were reportedly minors, adding another layer of complexity to the humanitarian crisis.

By Friday evening, Spanish authorities reported that a significant majority of the migrants had been returned to Morocco, facilitated by agreements between the two nations. However, the event has ignited a diplomatic firestorm. Morocco has been largely silent on the precise reasons for the lax border control, but speculation points to a dispute over Western Sahara, a territory claimed by both Morocco and the Polisario Front, which is supported by Algeria. Spain recently allowed the Polisario Front leader, Brahim Ghali, to receive medical treatment in Spain, a move that reportedly angered Rabat. The dramatic events in Ceuta underscore the persistent challenges of irregular migration and the complex geopolitical dynamics at play between Spain and Morocco.
