A wave of unexplained animal deaths and attacks plaguing rural villages across the country has been linked to a burgeoning illegal hunting trade, a BBC investigation has revealed. Farmers and landowners have reported a dramatic increase in livestock mutilations and the carcasses of dead animals found in fields, sparking fear and a sense of helplessness. These incidents, once dismissed as isolated acts, are now understood to be symptomatic of a far larger and more organized criminal enterprise: illegal hare coursing. 

The practice, which involves using greyhounds or other sighthounds to chase and kill hares, has been banned in the UK for over a decade. Despite its illegality, the BBC has uncovered evidence suggesting that nearly 9,000 reports related to banned hare coursing have been filed with police forces nationwide in recent years. This staggering figure underscores the scale of the problem, which often goes unreported or unaddressed due to the transient nature of the perpetrators and the difficulty in gathering evidence.

Organized groups, often traveling long distances, are believed to be involved in these clandestine hunts. They are attracted by the thrill of the chase and the opportunity to bet on the outcome. The financial incentives, though illicit, appear substantial enough to sustain a clandestine network. The consequences for rural communities are devastating. Beyond the emotional toll of witnessing such brutality, farmers face significant financial losses due to damaged livestock and property. The welfare of the dogs involved in coursing is also a grave concern, with many subjected to harsh training and treatment. As authorities grapple with the challenge of policing vast rural areas and prosecuting offenders, the mystery of the dead animals is slowly unraveling, exposing a disturbing hidden world operating in the shadows.
