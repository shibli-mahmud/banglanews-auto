India's economic trajectory has been a subject of much international admiration, often painted as a success story of rapid liberalization and growth. However, a closer examination reveals a nuanced picture, one characterized by a 'top-heavy' boom. This means that while the headline GDP figures are impressive, the benefits of this growth have not been equitably distributed. A significant portion of this expansion has been driven by large, established corporations, often benefiting from favorable government policies and access to capital. This has, in turn, contributed to a widening wealth gap, with a smaller segment of the population accumulating a disproportionate amount of wealth.

The consequences of such a growth model are manifold. It can lead to social stratification, increased inequality, and a potential dampening of domestic demand as a large part of the population struggles with purchasing power. Furthermore, an over-reliance on a few giant entities can create systemic risks, making the economy vulnerable to the fortunes of these major players. For developing economies like Bangladesh, which are striving for sustained and inclusive growth, India's experience serves as a potent reminder. While attracting investment and fostering large businesses are essential, policymakers must ensure that the growth strategy actively promotes broad-based economic participation. This involves nurturing small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), investing in human capital through education and skill development, and implementing progressive tax policies to redistribute wealth. The ultimate goal should be a development model that lifts all segments of society, not just those at the apex.
