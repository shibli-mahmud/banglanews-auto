The long-sought reforms, aimed at allowing authorities to detain offenders for longer periods in specific circumstances, have been a cornerstone of Burnham's justice agenda. Supporters argue these changes are vital for public safety, ensuring that dangerous individuals do not benefit from early release when their rehabilitation is deemed insufficient.  The government claims these adjustments will result in hundreds of additional inmates remaining behind bars, directly addressing public anxieties about recidivism and the perceived leniency of the justice system.

However, the practicalities of enacting these changes are far from straightforward. Sources within the Ministry of Justice indicate that implementing the new criteria will necessitate significant legislative drafting and potentially costly overhauls to the parole board system. Furthermore, the increased prison population will inevitably strain already stretched resources, from accommodation and staffing to healthcare and educational programs within correctional facilities. Critics question whether the government has adequately budgeted for these consequences, warning of potential overspending and a further deterioration of prison conditions.  The debate now shifts from the principle of reform to the practicalities of execution, with the true cost of Burnham's 'success' likely to become apparent in the coming months and years.
