Rural communities across the UK are grappling with an alarming rise in both outright attacks on livestock and game, and the unsettling discovery of dead animals. These incidents, initially appearing as isolated tragedies, have now been linked to a pervasive and organized criminal activity: illegal hare coursing. Farmers, the frontline victims, speak of their anguish and frustration as they discover their animals mutilated or killed, often with no clear explanation. The BBC has uncovered nearly 9,000 reports of banned hare coursing incidents in recent years, painting a stark picture of the widespread nature of this illegal sport.

Hare coursing, which involves using sighthounds to chase and kill hares, has been illegal in the UK since 2004. However, organized groups continue to operate, often travelling significant distances to find suitable hunting grounds. These groups are known to be highly mobile and secretive, making them difficult for law enforcement to track and apprehend. The dumping of dead animals, while seemingly disconnected, is now understood to be a byproduct of these illicit hunts, perhaps an attempt to dispose of evidence or simply a callous disregard for the creatures involved. The economic and emotional toll on farmers is substantial, impacting their livelihoods and sense of security. Authorities are struggling to keep pace with the sophistication of these criminal networks, which are often involved in other illegal activities. This investigation shines a light on a clandestine world of illegal hunts that is leaving a trail of destruction in its wake, demanding a more robust and coordinated response.
