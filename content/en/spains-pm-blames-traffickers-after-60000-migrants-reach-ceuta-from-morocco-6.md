The small Spanish North African enclave of Ceuta found itself overwhelmed earlier this week as a staggering number of migrants, estimated at around 60,000, managed to enter its territory from neighbouring Morocco. Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez has pointed the finger squarely at "traffickers and adventurers" who he claims exploited the situation, suggesting the surge was a deliberate tactic by Morocco, though Rabat has denied this. The sheer volume of arrivals, many of whom were young men and some children, quickly overwhelmed local resources and sparked a humanitarian concern.

Spanish authorities initiated a rapid repatriation process, with reports indicating that a significant majority of the migrants had been returned to Morocco by Friday evening. The swiftness of these returns, however, has raised questions about the handling of the situation and the international obligations of both Spain and Morocco. 

Adding to the diplomatic fallout, Italy has announced the suspension of its Schengen agreement on free movement with Madrid. Italian Interior Minister Luciana Lamorgese stated that the decision was made "in agreement with the President of the Council of Ministers" due to concerns over border management and the potential for secondary movements of migrants within the EU. This move by Italy underscores the anxieties felt by some European nations regarding the security of external borders and the impact of large-scale irregular migration. The incident in Ceuta highlights the persistent challenges faced by Spain and the broader European Union in managing migration flows and combating the activities of human smugglers.
