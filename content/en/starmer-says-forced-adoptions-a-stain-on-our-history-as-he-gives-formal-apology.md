During a significant parliamentary address, Keir Starmer, leader of the Labour Party, formally apologised for the historic practice of forced adoptions in the United Kingdom. He described the period, predominantly spanning from 1949 to 1976, as a 'stain on our history' and acknowledged the profound and lasting pain inflicted upon countless mothers and families. The practice involved the state, often through child welfare agencies and maternity homes, taking babies from unmarried mothers and young women, and placing them with adoptive families, frequently without the birth mothers' genuine consent or against their wishes.

Starmer's apology comes after decades of campaigning by survivors and advocacy groups who have worked tirelessly to bring these injustices to light. The government's formal recognition of this historical wrong is seen as a crucial step towards acknowledging the suffering endured by those affected. Many women were pressured or coerced into giving up their babies, often facing social stigma and a lack of support. The long-term consequences for both mothers and their children have been devastating, leading to decades of separation, grief, and unanswered questions. The apology aims to offer some measure of comfort and validation to those who were harmed, and to ensure that such practices are never repeated.
