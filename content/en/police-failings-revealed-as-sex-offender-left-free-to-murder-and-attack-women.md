The Metropolitan Police's handling of serial sex offender Simon Levy has come under severe criticism following admissions that a catastrophic series of failings allowed him to commit further heinous crimes. Senior officers have now conceded that Sheryl Wilkins, Levy's second known murder victim, might still be alive had the police acted more effectively on the intelligence they possessed. Wilkins was murdered in 2002, five years after Levy had already been convicted of rape and indecent assault in 1997. Despite these convictions, Levy was not considered a significant enough risk to warrant closer monitoring or detention, a decision that has now been retrospectively condemned.

An ongoing independent inquiry is meticulously reviewing the circumstances surrounding Levy's offending, and its preliminary findings point to a systemic breakdown in communication and risk management within the Met. Evidence suggests that vital information regarding Levy's continued predatory behaviour was not adequately shared between departments or acted upon with the urgency required. The admission by a senior officer marks a significant moment, acknowledging the profound impact of these oversights on the lives of victims and their families. The report is expected to detail specific recommendations to prevent similar tragedies in the future, focusing on improving intelligence sharing, perpetrator risk assessment, and victim safeguarding protocols. The Met has stated it is committed to implementing any changes deemed necessary to restore public trust.
